Debt consolidation

When debt consolidation belongs on the house, and when it does not

November 3, 2026 · Mark Heeney, Mortgage Agent Level 2

Rolling credit cards or a car loan into a mortgage can lower the monthly payment. It can also move debt that used to be unsecured onto the house, and stretch a short balance across a much longer amortization. A smaller payment is not the same thing as a cheaper debt.

Why this matters now

People usually ask about consolidation when the cards are loud and the mortgage renewal, or a refinance, is already on the table. That is a reasonable question. It is a bad question to answer from the payment alone.

A practical checklist

  • Write each balance, its interest rate, and the months left if you paid more than the minimum.
  • Ask what the total interest would be if those balances stayed where they are and were paid off on a short timeline.
  • Ask what the total interest would be if the same dollars were added to the mortgage and paid over the remaining amortization.
  • Check whether the new mortgage payment still fits after property tax, heat, and the spending that created the cards.
  • Decide, before you sign, what stops the cards from filling back up. A consolidation without a spending change recreates the debt and keeps the larger mortgage.

Common mistakes

  • Comparing only the monthly payment.
  • Securing high-interest debt against the home without a plan to pay it off faster than the mortgage schedule.
  • Treating a refinance as a reset that makes last year’s spending fine.

Where a mortgage conversation fits

Sometimes a refinance or a second mortgage is the cleaner path, especially when the cards are blocking a purchase or a renewal. Sometimes a smaller consolidation loan, or a payoff plan that leaves the house alone, costs less. Mark can walk through the structure. Approval and pricing depend on the lender and the file at the time.
